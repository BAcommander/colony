- A dominant weather condition should consistently reduce distant visibility, contrast and saturation. Leaving every object equally legible weakens depth.
- Use ordinary functional construction, a camera with a plausible place to stand, traceable services and continuous access. Design a place people maintain, allowing modest asymmetry and practical inconveniences without unsafe or impossible routes.
- Preserve broad plain walls, empty sky and other quiet areas. Concentrate detail around actual work and contact/weather exposure; do not distribute sharp texture uniformly.
- Restrict the palette through shared illumination and material colour. One modest warm refuge can carry habitation; numerous glowing windows and screens can compete.

- Build the alien identity into a large environmental form, a consistent supporting cue and an engineering response. Earth scenery plus a small moon remains insufficient. Do not trade realism for spectacular gadgets or neon geology.

## The central change

Start with a place people need in order to live here, then make the unfamiliar environment shape that place. A colony is a network of homes, work, food, water, power and transport. It can be a small settlement or an established district; it need not be three identical pods pasted onto a horizon.

Each concept needs a legible combination of:

1. **One environmental signature:** a memorable silhouette, landform, native growth form, ice structure, ocean condition or orbital view, visible at ordinary thumbnail scale.
2. **One supporting world cue:** consistent material, atmosphere or ecological behaviour rather than an unrelated spectacle.
3. **One construction consequence:** the settlement's foundations, enclosure, connections or maintenance provisions visibly respond to that world.
4. **One sign of everyday habitation:** a repaired seat, occupied windows, a coat in a locker, a dry work surface, a meal tray or useful stored supplies. Choose a few, not all.

The image should work even if a small moon is hidden. A celestial object may be a defining subject in a deliberately orbital scene; it is not the universal fix for an otherwise terrestrial landscape.

## Photographic realism, written as constraints that can be checked

- Choose a specific standing/seated camera position and 35–50 mm full-frame-equivalent perspective. The camera must have somewhere to stand. Avoid exaggerated wide-angle furniture and miniature building scale.
- Compose a human-scale near area, useful connected middle distance and one dominant distant form. Keep portions of the frame genuinely quiet. A large view does not require a larger room.
- Specify supports, thresholds, doors and routes. Follow pipes to their destination. Keep tracks, bridges, stairs and landings continuous; simplify them if necessary.
- Give each object an identifiable material response. Chalk, rubber, cloth, brushed metal and painted panels should not all carry the same sharp glossy noise. Wear belongs where hands, water, grit and weather reach it.
- Expose for a believable balance of exterior and interior. Let recesses stay dark, not crushed. A few windows may be bright; every screen should not become a light source. No automatic orange sunset in every scene.
- Colour should belong to a material, atmosphere or fixture. A muted unfamiliar hue can be strong at large scale without being neon. Avoid uniformly spaced pink rock stripes and decorative rainbow strata.
- Repeat a native organism's basic structure with variation in age, orientation and damage. Do not mix palms, mushrooms, glowing vines and crystal flowers to signal “alien.” Do not enlarge an Earth houseplant and call the ecology solved.
- Keep a real settlement legible: a route reaches a door, a power line supplies a district, a utility plant connects to storage or housing. One connected inhabited complex can be enough; do not demand a distant pod cluster in every shot.
- Filmic does not mean grunge, HDR, starbursts, universal wetness or extreme shallow focus. Give windows faint plausible reflections; no duplicated planets or reflected objects with no source.

## Still review before thumbnail or animation

1. At full composition, identify the environmental signature and the construction response without reading the title.
2. Hide a small celestial cue mentally: does the setting retain a distinct identity? Check this especially for exterior concepts.
3. Can a person enter, sit, work, reach equipment and get home? Follow major supports and access routes.
4. Inspect high-risk objects: cup handles, chairs, keyboards, rails/switches, bridge anchors, pipe joins, stairs and light fixtures. Prefer a handleless beaker over repeated mug repairs.
5. Check atmospheric and lighting consistency across all windows and surfaces. Verify scene-specific air/water/pressure assumptions; sealed windows stay sealed.
6. Compare against the last three scenes: does this merely repeat dark rubble, amber sunset and three pods? If so change composition or landform before tuning colour.
7. Confirm no copied title/logo/UI; verify the clean composition has useful title space without redesigning it into a poster.
8. Plan later motion around one or two broad, maskable regions already present. Do not deform geology, invent objects or fill the frame with fine particles to manufacture activity.

Record an honest result: the concrete success, largest defect, next targeted revision and the user's actual verdict. Generated, inspected and accepted remain separate states.
